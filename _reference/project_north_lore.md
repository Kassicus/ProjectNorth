# PROJECT NORTH — Lore Bible
**Version 0.1 — Working Draft** · Companion to project_north_gdd.md (§7)

> **Decided framework [CORE]:** Man-made ecological damage exposed and awakened an ancient power that is slowly enacting its reclamation. Fully fictional cosmology, Nordic-themed. All invented names, invented folklore, invented peoples — no real-world Indigenous cultures depicted, referenced, or borrowed; the "old country" material is likewise a fictionalized pseudo-Scandinavia, not real Norse mythology.
>
> Everything below builds that framework out. **[PROPOSAL]** marks specific choices to react to; **[OPEN]** marks decisions still needed. This is the *design-side truth* — the player learns it in fragments, out of order, and never all of it.

---

## 1. Cosmology — The Old Winter **[PROPOSAL]**

Before winter was a season, it was a **territory**.

In the deep past of this fiction, the cold that ringed the top of the world was not weather but *domain* — a sovereign power with borders, terms, and a slow, vast attention. It did not think the way people think. It kept accounts the way ice keeps a shoreline: patiently, completely, without anger. The oldest word the game's fictional peoples have for it translates roughly as **the Vintermark** — *the winter-march*, the borderland of winter — naming both the power and the land it claims. (Naming options in §8.)

The Vintermark is not a god and was never worshipped. It is closer to a **landlord of last resort**: everything in the boreal ring lived, in the oldest understanding, *on its land, under terms.* Take only what regrows. Return what you owe. Keep the marked boundaries. Do not dig below the frost that never melts. In exchange, winter *ends* — every year, on time, the domain withdraws and lends the land back.

Crucially: the Vintermark is **contractual, not cruel.** It has never broken terms. It cannot. That is the horror of it — what is happening to the world is not revenge in the human sense. It is **enforcement**. The lease was violated, and the landlord is repossessing.

*(Design intent: this reframe is the Act 4 revelation that recontextualizes everything — pillar 5 in the GDD. "Winter is the antagonist" becomes "winter is owed." The player's journey is from fighting it to understanding the terms — and discovering the terms can still be kept.)*

## 2. The Fence-Builders — Deep History **[PROPOSAL]**

The fictional early peoples of the boreal ring — the game names them only in fragments, as later folklore does; provisional term **the Stavfolk** ("stave-folk," for the marked posts and stones they raised) — learned the terms the hard way across generations, and did something remarkable: they **wrote the contract into the land itself.**

- **Wardstones** (provisional: **vardstenar**): standing stones cut with term-marks, raised at the domain's boundary lines. Not magical barriers — *boundary markers with witness*. A wardstone is a clause made permanent. While a stone stands tended, the terms it records hold on that land.
- **Observances**: small seasonal acts of accounting — first-catch returned to the water, first-fell timber left to rot where it fell, silence kept on the longest night, the boundary walked and the stones swept after every thaw. Not worship. **Bookkeeping.** Each observance re-affirms a clause.
- **Keepers**: as the Stavfolk's world changed and scattered, the general practice narrowed to designated families — **stone-keepers** (provisional: **stenvakter**) — who tended stones and kept observances on behalf of everyone else. The wider culture forgot; the keepers kept. Over centuries, keeping became hereditary, secret, and increasingly misunderstood even by the keepers themselves — clauses maintained as superstition, the *why* eroding, the *what* surviving in family habit and hand-copied books.

By the modern era of the fiction, the Vintermark's waking domain had contracted to the deep polar north, and its attention had settled into something like sleep — not because it weakened, but because **the terms were being kept** at enough of the old boundary stones, by enough scattered keeper families, on both sides of the Atlantic. Winter came and went on schedule. The world mistook the contract for climate.

**[OPEN]** How much of the Stavfolk material do we show vs. imply? Recommend: never a flashback, never a text-dump — only stones, marks, keeper marginalia, and the pilot's half-remembered settlement folklore.

## 3. The Two Shores **[PROPOSAL]**

Core worldbuilding move (this is what makes the Nordic theming *work* in Canada without borrowing any real culture):

**The Vintermark is circumpolar. The boreal ring is one territory, and it always was.** The Stavfolk ranged its whole circumference in the deep past; wardstones stand — mostly lost, buried, or drowned — across the entire top of the world. The keeper tradition survived best in the game's fictional pseudo-Scandinavia (provisional name for the old country region: **Nordheim** — deliberately generic; see §8), but the stones themselves are everywhere the old domain touched.

So when grandfather — a stone-keeper's son from Nordheim — emigrated to Canada as a young man to escape the keeping, and stumbled onto a ridge above a nameless lake and saw **the same term-marks his grandmother swept every spring an ocean away**, he understood three things at once:

1. The keeping was never about his family's valley. It was about the whole ring.
2. This boundary had had no keeper for a very, very long time.
3. He could never go home, and he could never leave.

*(This is the emotional spine of his character: he fled the contract and it was waiting for him on the other side of the world.)*

**[OPEN]** Confirm this "fled the old country, found the same stones" backstory — it explains the Nordic register organically (his journals, his carvings, his loanwords seeded through the game's terminology) and makes the cosmology genuinely circumpolar rather than transplanted.

## 4. The Wound **[PROPOSAL — pick the specific damage]**

The local violation that broke the terms. Candidates, all period-plausible for mid-century wilderness Canada:

- **A. The Mine (recommended):** an exploratory hard-rock mine sunk in the late 1950s on the far side of the lake's watershed — active a decade, then abandoned when the vein disappointed. It did the one thing the oldest clause forbids: **it dug below the frost that never melts.** The shafts pierced permafrost that the terms name as the Vintermark's own body — *the part of the domain that never gets lent back.* Tailings still leach into the feeder creek; the ice on that arm of the lake has always frozen strangely. Grandfather's second journal calls the mine **the Wound** and the deep workings **the Undervint** ("the under-winter"). Gameplay: a genuinely explorable late-game site — flooded levels, frozen machinery, term-marks scratched *inside* by someone (who? — hook), and the deepest place in the game.
- **B. The Dam:** a hydro project drowned a boundary valley and eleven wardstones in the 1960s. The stones still stand underwater, terms intact but untendable. Winter has been "reading" the drowned clauses ever since, slowly. Beautiful imagery (diving/ice-hole descent to a drowned stone), but weaker "extraction" theming.
- **C. The Cut:** industrial clearcut logging of a warded old-growth stand; the fastest-healing wound (regrowth) and thus the weakest.

**Recommendation: A**, with B's drowned-stone *imagery* stolen for a single flooded mine level. The mine gives us extraction-era ecological damage (theme-perfect), a dungeon, a mystery (the interior marks), and a concrete reason the seal *here* is the world's most broken.

**[OPEN]** Is the local Wound uniquely bad, or just one of many? Recommend: many wounds worldwide (hence the hemispheric ice age — every unkept boundary failing), but this one is *special* because it pierced the Undervint **and** because it sits at a boundary that still has a keeper-line — which is why the world's fate can plausibly hinge on the player without breaking scale.

## 5. Grandfather — The Last Keeper **[PROPOSAL]**

Working chronology (name **[OPEN]** — see §8 candidates):

- Born in Nordheim to a stenvakter family; raised sweeping stones and keeping silences he was never fully told the reasons for. Resented it. Emigrated young — the classic mid-century crossing — to be *free* of it.
- Finds the ridge. Recognizes the marks. Understands. Buys the land with everything he has (the estate the player inherits) and builds the cabin **inside the boundary, at the threshold** — keepers live on the line, not behind it.
- Decades of quiet keeping: the observances, the boundary walks, the stones swept after thaw. His first journal is homestead wisdom; the second — begun the year the mine's effects first showed in the ice — is the keeping itself, increasingly desperate, increasingly *illegible* (see Attunement, GDD §6.2: the scrawl is not madness; late keeper-writing shifts into term-marks, readable only with attunement).
- He works for years on **the unfinished thing in the workshop [OPEN]** — recommend: a **new wardstone**, cut by hand to carry a clause that would answer the Wound; unfinished because a stone must be *raised and witnessed* by a keeper, and he knew he was dying. The player finishing and raising it is a late-game mainline beat and a perfect fusion of the crafting game and the mystery game.
- Knowing the keeping dies with him, he makes his bet: **pre-pays the pilot for a year of weekly flights**, names his estranged kin as heir, and seeds both journals where curiosity will find them. The keeping passes by **blood or threshold** — by inheriting the line, or by living on the boundary and taking up the terms. The player qualifies twice.
- **[OPEN]** How did he die, and where? (In the game's world, before the player arrives.) Options: quietly of age mid-observance at the ridge (found by the pilot — gives the pilot a haunted first-hand scene to eventually share); or *unknown* — no body, an open question the player can resolve. The second is stronger mystery but must be handled so it doesn't read as "he's secretly alive" cheap.

## 6. The Keeping — Observances as Mechanics **[PROPOSAL]**

The bridge between lore and gameplay (GDD §6.3's "rituals over spells," firmed up):

- The player never casts. The player **keeps terms.** The verb set is homestead-shaped: *sweep* (tend a stone), *return* (give back a first-portion of any harvest type), *walk* (patrol the boundary), *keep silence* (a timed night-observance), *witness* (be present at a site at the right sky/date), *raise* (the endgame stone).
- Observances are learned from the second journal as attunement grows, and *they visibly work*: a kept clause calms local anomalies, steadies weather at the homestead, quiets the dreams. An unkept one lets the wrongness in closer. The homestead becomes a **negotiated pocket** in the deepening winter — the player is not beating the Vintermark, they are *back in good standing*, hyper-locally.
- This makes Act 4's mainline legible: extend good standing outward, stone by stone, clause by clause, toward the Wound — then answer the Wound itself (the raised stone, the Undervint descent, the final terms).
- **Design guardrail:** observances must never become a chore-list that trivializes the winter. They are few, deliberate, and calendar-meaningful — more like Stardew's community center by way of a legal ritual than a daily buff rotation.

**[OPEN]** Does keeping ever *fail* or backfire (mis-kept terms making things worse), or is it purely restorative? Backfire risk adds tension but needs careful signaling.

## 7. The Endgame & Endings — Lore Alignment **[OPEN — select]**

What "solving" the winter means under this cosmology: the ice age is **many boundaries failing at once**; the player cannot re-fence the world. But the terms have a provision the second journal circles obsessively — provisional clause name **the Hovedsten** ("head-stone"): a boundary kept *perfectly*, by a keeper who **chose** the keeping with full understanding (not inherited blindly), stands witness for the whole territory. One fully honored contract re-establishes the *precedent*; the Vintermark, contractual to its core, must then offer terms again everywhere before it repossesses further.

This scales the endings from the GDD:
- **The Warden:** the player becomes the standing witness; the world gets its offer of terms (winter recedes); the witness must remain at the boundary. The returning plane lands on a spring lake.
- **The Restitution:** the final terms are priced by the player's whole playthrough — everything taken vs. returned, kept vs. skipped (the game has silently been an account book all along). Pay the balance, in a currency to be designed **[OPEN]**, and walk away free.
- **The Thaw / The Refusal:** as in the GDD.

**[OPEN]** One canonical ending vs. player choice among these; and whether the Refusal path gets its own quiet recognition (recommend yes — a final journal page, nothing more).

## 9. What the Player Learns, and When **[PROPOSAL — the drip schedule]**

| Act | Player's model of the truth |
|---|---|
| 1 | "I'm imagining things. Grandfather was a quirky old homesteader." |
| 2 | "Grandfather believed something was here. These stones are real. Something is wrong with the world's weather." (Second journal found; mostly illegible.) |
| 3 | "It's all one thing — the stones, the winter, the mine. He was *keeping* something, and it stopped being kept." (False Thaw; attunement opens the journal; the Wound identified.) |
| 4 | "It isn't malice. It's a contract. And it can still be kept." (The Undervint; the Hovedsten clause; the unfinished stone; the choice.) |

Guardrail: every act's model should be *reasonable given what's shown* — the player should never feel the game withheld arbitrarily, only that the land gives up its terms slowly, to those who keep them.
